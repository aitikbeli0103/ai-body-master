Migraine

A strong, throbbing headache, often on one side, that can come with sickness and sensitivity to light

ConditionBrain & nervesHead

What it is

Migraine is a headache condition. Attacks of strong, throbbing pain, often on one side of the head, can last hours to days and may come with feeling sick or bothered by light and noise.

  • Head, often one side
  • More common in women
  • Throbbing, light-sensitive
Which doctor
Family doctor; brain and nerve doctor (neurologist) if needed
Common in
Often starts from the teens to middle age; more often in women
Contagious
No
Course
Comes and goes in attacks; knowing your triggers may help

Common signs

  • Throbbing or pulsing pain, often on one side of the head
  • Feeling sick or vomiting during an attack
  • Light, noise or smells feel too strong
  • Some people see flashing lights, zigzag lines or blind spots just before the pain (aura)
  • Feeling tired, moody or yawning a lot a day or so before an attack

Why it happens

  • The exact cause is not fully known; it often runs in families
  • Triggers differ for each person, such as stress, too little or too much sleep, or skipped meals
  • Hormone changes, for example around the period
  • Strong smells, bright or flickering light, loud noise or weather changes
  • Some foods or drinks, such as alcohol, in some people

See a doctor first if

  • A sudden, very severe headache, the worst of your life: call 999
  • Headache with face drooping, arm weakness, speech trouble or sudden loss of vision: call 999
  • Headache with fever and a stiff neck, a rash, confusion or a fit: go to hospital now
  • A headache after a knock to the head, with vomiting or drowsiness: go to hospital now
  • A new kind of headache later in life, or a sudden change in your usual pattern: see a doctor soon
  • Pregnant with a bad headache or blurred vision: see a doctor the same day

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

This page is general information. Please keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Looking after yourself

  • During an attack, rest in a quiet, dark room and put a cool cloth on your forehead
  • Keep a diary of your attacks to spot your triggers, and show it to your doctor
  • Keep regular sleep and meal times, drink enough water and manage stress gently
  • Ask your doctor or pharmacist about pain relief; using it too often can make headaches worse
  • Stay gently active, for example by walking, when you feel well between attacks

Tell your therapist

  • Recent neck injury or surgery
  • Dizzy when turning the head
  • High blood pressure
  • Pregnancy
  • Blood-thinning medicine

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
