Frozen shoulder

Also called adhesive capsulitis

ConditionBones, joints & spineArms & legsBones & muscles

What it is

A painful, stiff shoulder where the tissue around the joint becomes tight and thick, so the arm moves less and less.

  • Shoulder joint
  • Most often 40 to 60
  • Painful and stiff
Which doctor
Family doctor, orthopaedic doctor, physiotherapist
Common in
Adults aged 40 to 60, more often women
Contagious
No
Course
Slow: often 1 to 3 years, in three stages
Medical details

What happens in the shoulder

The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint wrapped in a thin, flexible capsule. In frozen shoulder the capsule becomes inflamed, then thick and tight, and forms scar-like bands (adhesions); there is also less joint fluid. The joint can no longer move through its normal range, and every attempt to stretch it hurts. It usually affects one shoulder; the other shoulder is sometimes affected later.

The three stages

  • Freezing: pain slowly increases, is worse at night, and movement starts to shrink; lasts about 2 to 9 months
  • Frozen: the pain may ease, but the shoulder is very stiff; lasts about 4 to 12 months
  • Thawing: movement slowly returns over about 6 months to 2 years

Outlook

Frozen shoulder usually gets better on its own over 1 to 3 years, but it can take a long time. Gentle regular movement, pain relief chosen by your doctor and physiotherapy can make the course more comfortable. Some people are left with a little stiffness.

Common signs

Medical details

Symptoms in more detail

  • Pain: a dull or aching pain deep in the shoulder, sometimes spreading into the upper arm; worse at night and when you lie on the shoulder
  • Stiffness: the arm cannot lift to the side or forward, or turn outward or behind the back; someone else cannot move it much either, which is a key sign of a frozen shoulder
  • Everyday tasks: combing hair, dressing, fastening a bra or belt, and reaching a shelf or a seat belt become hard
  • Not typical: numbness or tingling down the arm, neck pain that is worse than the shoulder pain, or a hot, red, swollen shoulder need a doctor's check

Why it happens

Medical details

Causes and risk factors

Often there is no clear cause. Frozen shoulder is linked with long periods when the arm is not moved, and with some health conditions.

  • Age and sex: mostly between 40 and 60, and more often in women
  • Little movement: after a fracture, an operation, a stroke or a long illness, or when the arm is kept still for a long time
  • Diabetes: people with diabetes are more likely to develop it and may recover more slowly
  • Other conditions: an underactive or overactive thyroid, heart or lung disease and some nerve conditions
  • Earlier shoulder injury: a strain or a rotator cuff injury can lead to a stiff shoulder

See a doctor first if

  • Shoulder pain after a fall or injury
  • Pain with chest tightness, sweating or breathlessness: call 999
  • A hot, red or swollen shoulder, or a fever
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the arm or hand
  • Severe night pain that you cannot manage
  • A shoulder not improving after a few weeks

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Medical details

How doctors check it

  • Examination: the doctor moves your arm in each direction and compares how far it goes on its own and with help; in frozen shoulder both are limited
  • X-ray: usually normal in frozen shoulder; used to rule out arthritis, a fracture or calcium deposits
  • Ultrasound or MRI: can show the tendons and the capsule, mainly to rule out a rotator cuff tear

Problems that can feel similar

Neck problems, a rotator cuff tear, shoulder arthritis and, rarely, heart problems can cause shoulder pain, so a doctor checks to tell them apart.

Pain with chest tightness, sweating or breathlessness can be a heart problem: call emergency services (999).

Looking after yourself

Medical details

Daily care in more detail

  • Warm up first: a warm shower or a warm pack for about 10 minutes before stretching relaxes the muscles
  • Gentle stretches: pendulum swings, finger walks up a wall and towel stretches, a few short sessions a day, within a comfortable range and never forced
  • Keep using the arm: do everyday tasks within the range that does not give sharp pain; a long rest makes the shoulder stiffer
  • Sleep: lie on your good side with a pillow in front to support the sore arm, or on your back with the arm on a pillow
  • Pain relief: ask your doctor or pharmacist what is safe for you; do not start new medicine on your own
  • Physiotherapy: a physiotherapist can guide the right exercises for each stage; too much stretching in the early painful stage can make it worse

Tell your therapist

  • Recent shoulder injury or surgery
  • Hot, red or swollen shoulder
  • Numb or tingling arm
  • Weak bones
  • Pacemaker or metal implant
  • Diabetes or less feeling in the skin

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
