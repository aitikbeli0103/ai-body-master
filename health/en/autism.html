Autism

A lifelong difference in how the brain works that affects communication, social ways and behaviour

ConditionTold by a doctor: attention & developmentWhole body

What it is

Autism is a lifelong difference in how the brain develops. It can affect how a person communicates, relates to others and senses the world. It is a spectrum, so each person is different.

  • Brain development
  • Usually seen in early childhood
  • Communication and senses differ
Which doctor
Paediatrician, family doctor, psychiatrist or psychologist
Common in
Usually starts in early childhood; some people are only found as adults
Contagious
No
Course
Lifelong; early help and steady support make daily life easier for many

Common signs

  • Little eye contact, or finding it hard to join in with other people
  • Speech that comes late or sounds different, or repeating words and phrases
  • Repeating movements, such as hand flapping or rocking
  • A strong need for routine, and distress when things change
  • Strong reactions to sounds, lights, touch or tastes

Why it happens

Other causes

  • Being born very early, or having older parents, may raise the chance
  • It is not caused by vaccines, and not by bad parenting

See a doctor first if

  • Thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life: call 999 or go to hospital now
  • Hurting themselves or others, or running into danger such as a road: call 999
  • A fit (seizure) that is new or does not stop quickly: call 999
  • A child who does not respond to their name, does not speak, or loses words or skills: see a doctor promptly
  • Meltdowns that cannot be calmed and keep happening, or not eating, drinking or sleeping: see a doctor soon
  • Low mood, strong worry or staying away from everyone for weeks: see a doctor soon

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

This page is general information. Please keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Looking after yourself

  • Ask a doctor about an assessment early; early support helps many children
  • Keep a clear, steady daily routine; pictures or written plans can help
  • Use short, clear words and give time to answer
  • Reduce loud noise and bright lights; give a quiet place to rest
  • Join a support group, and keep up school and family support programmes

Tell your therapist

  • Epilepsy or fits
  • Pregnancy
  • Pacemaker or metal implant

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
