Depigmentation (loss of skin colour)

Patches of skin that lose their normal colour and look lighter or white

ConditionSkin, hair & nailsSkin

What it is

Part of the skin loses its colour and looks lighter or white next to the skin around it. There are many causes, some harmless, and a doctor can tell them apart.

  • Skin on any part of the body
  • Can start at any age
  • Lighter or white patches
Which doctor
Skin doctor (dermatologist); family doctor first; children's doctor for a child
Common in
Any age; some causes run in families or follow eczema, burns or a skin infection
Contagious
Some causes can be, such as certain skin infections; vitiligo is not
Course
Depends on the cause; some patches may regain colour, others stay or spread slowly

Common signs

  • Patches of skin that look lighter or white compared with the skin around them
  • Patches that are often seen on the face, hands, feet or around the eyes and mouth
  • Usually no pain, though some patches are dry, scaly or a little itchy
  • Hair growing in the patch may turn white too
  • The pale skin can burn more easily in the sun

Why it happens

Other causes

  • Vitiligo, when the skin's colour-making cells are lost; often the reason is not known
  • Skin that was inflamed, burned or injured, for example after eczema or a rash
  • A fungal skin infection (such as tinea versicolor) that leaves paler patches
  • Some thyroid or other body-defence (autoimmune) conditions are linked with vitiligo

See a doctor first if

  • A pale patch that has lost feeling, or numb or weak hands or feet: see a doctor promptly
  • A patch that grows quickly, changes shape or colour, bleeds or becomes a sore
  • Loss of colour that is spreading fast, or any new white patch in a child
  • Patches together with tiredness, weight change, or feeling too hot or too cold

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

This page is general information. Please keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Looking after yourself

More ways to look after yourself

  • Be gentle with the skin: avoid scratching, rubbing and injuries, which can trigger patches in some people
  • Do not use bleaching creams, unlabelled creams or home remedies on the patches
  • Take photos now and then to see whether the patches change
  • Talk to someone you trust if it worries you; support is available

Tell your therapist

  • Broken, red or sore skin
  • Cancer or cancer treatment
  • Diabetes or less feeling in the skin

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
