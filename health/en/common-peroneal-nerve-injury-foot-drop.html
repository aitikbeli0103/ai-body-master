Common peroneal nerve injury (foot drop)

Damage to the nerve that runs by the outer knee and lifts the foot, so the foot drags

ConditionPelvis, hip, knee & legArms & legs

What it is

The common peroneal nerve runs close to the skin at the outer side of the knee. If it is pressed or hurt, the foot may be hard to lift, which is called foot drop.

  • Outer side of the knee
  • After pressure or injury
  • Foot drags, hard to lift
Which doctor
Family doctor, nerve doctor (neurologist), bone doctor
Common in
After knee injuries or breaks; people who cross legs a lot or are very thin
Contagious
No
Course
Depends on the cause; some people improve over weeks to months, others need longer

Common signs

More signs

  • The front of the foot slaps down or drags when walking
  • Lifting the knee higher than usual to keep the toes off the floor
  • Weak ankle or toes when pulling the foot up
  • Tripping or falling more often

Why it happens

Other causes

  • Pressure on the outer knee, such as crossing legs, a tight cast or long kneeling
  • A knee injury, knee dislocation or a broken bone near the knee

See a doctor first if

  • Sudden weakness in the leg together with face drooping, arm weakness or speech trouble: call 999
  • Sudden foot drop after an injury or accident: go to hospital
  • Weakness or numbness spreading up the leg, or trouble passing urine or stools
  • Numbness that keeps getting worse, or a painful swollen leg
  • Falling or tripping often

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

This page is general information. Please keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Looking after yourself

  • Avoid crossing your legs and avoid pressing on the outer knee
  • Watch your step, use good lighting and wear shoes that fit well
  • Ask your doctor about a foot support or walking aid
  • Keep your blood sugar well controlled if you have diabetes
  • Do the movement exercises your care team recommends

Tell your therapist

  • Numb or tingling leg or foot
  • Weak leg or foot
  • Diabetes or less feeling in the skin
  • Pacemaker or metal implant
  • Blood-thinning medicine

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
