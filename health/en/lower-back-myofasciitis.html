Lower back myofasciitis

A dull, spreading ache across the lower back from tight, irritated muscles and the tissue around them

Medical details

What happens in the muscles and fascia

Several layers of muscle cover the lower back: the long muscles that run beside the spine (the erector spinae), the deep muscle at each side of the waist (the quadratus lumborum) and, over them, a broad sheet of fascia called the thoracolumbar fascia. Fascia is the thin, tough tissue that wraps and connects muscles. When these muscles are strained, kept in one position for hours or chilled, small patches of muscle fibre can stay tightened. They form tight bands with tender knots (myofascial trigger points) that hurt when touched and can send a dull ache across the back or into the buttock. Despite the name, true inflammation is not always present, which is why doctors often say myofascial pain. The nerves and discs are usually not the main problem, and scans are often normal.

Common patterns

  • After a strain: a lift, a twist or a day of unusual work leaves the area sore and tight; it usually settles over days to weeks
  • Long-lasting: an ache that keeps returning for months, often worse in cold or damp weather, in an air-conditioned room, after long sitting and when you are stressed
  • With a stiff morning back: the back feels stiff on waking and loosens as you move and warm up; stiffness that lasts longer than about 30 minutes every morning, or pain that wakes you at night, needs a doctor's check

Outlook

Most people feel better over a few weeks with movement, warmth, better posture and strengthening exercises. The ache can return after long sitting, a chill or a stressful period, and when it has lasted for months it tends to come and go. Treatment decisions are made by your doctor.

Common signs

Medical details

Symptoms in more detail

  • Dull, spreading ache: a deep, widespread ache across the lower back, often on both sides; it can spread to the buttock or hip but is usually not felt below the knee
  • Tender knots: tight bands or small knots in the muscles beside the spine or at the sides of the waist, sore when touched
  • Stiffness: the back is stiff on waking or after sitting, and eases as you move and the body warms up
  • Cold and weather: many people find the ache worse in cold, damp weather or in a cold air-conditioned room, and better with warmth
  • Limited bending: bending, twisting or standing up straight feels tight and uncomfortable
  • Not typical: numbness, weakness in a leg, pain that shoots below the knee, or trouble with urine or bowels. These need a doctor's check

Why it happens

Medical details

Causes and risk factors

Myofascial pain of the lower back usually starts when the muscles are loaded more than they can recover from: one strain, or the same position and movement repeated day after day. Cold and stress can keep the muscles tight.

  • Long sitting or standing: hours in one position keep the same muscles working without a rest
  • A sudden strain: lifting, twisting or an awkward movement; a small injury that never fully settles can leave tight patches
  • Poor posture: slouching, a chair without back support, or carrying loads on one side
  • Cold: cool air from an air-conditioner or a chill after sweating can make muscles tighten; the exact reason is not fully understood
  • Weak core and little exercise: tired muscles tighten more easily
  • Stress, poor sleep, extra weight and smoking: linked with more muscle pain and slower recovery

See a doctor first if

  • Numb around the groin or inner thighs, or new trouble passing urine or controlling the bowels: go to hospital now
  • A weak leg or foot
  • Back pain after a fall or accident
  • Pain with a fever, unexplained weight loss, or severe pain that wakes you at night
  • New back pain for the first time after about 50, or with a past cancer
  • Pain not easing after about 6 weeks

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Medical details

How doctors check it

Myofascial pain is diagnosed mainly from your history and examination, after other causes have been considered. Scans often show changes in people with no pain, so results are always read together with your symptoms.

  • History and examination: where and when it hurts, what eases or worsens it, and your work, sleep and stress; the doctor looks at posture and movement, feels the muscles for tight bands and tender knots, and checks leg strength, reflexes and feeling
  • Tender knot findings: a firm touch on a tight knot often brings on your familiar pain, sometimes with an ache that spreads to the buttock; no blood test or scan can confirm myofascial pain
  • X-ray, MRI or CT: not needed for most people; used when there are warning signs, nerve symptoms or pain that does not improve
  • Blood tests: only if an inflammatory condition or an infection is suspected

Problems that can feel similar

A slipped disc, sciatica, wear of the spine, sacroiliac joint pain, inflammatory back pain (a type of arthritis of the spine that causes morning stiffness, often in younger people) and kidney or urine infections can all cause lower-back pain, so a doctor checks to tell them apart. Back pain with a high fever, chest pain or a sudden severe belly pain is an emergency: call 999.

Looking after yourself

Medical details

Daily care in more detail

  • Warmth: a warm pack, a warm shower or a warm wrap on the lower back before you move can loosen tight muscles; check the heat on your skin and do not fall asleep on a heat pack; keep the lower back covered in an air-conditioned room and in cold weather
  • Move often: change position every 30 to 45 minutes and take short walks; gentle activity helps more than long bed rest
  • Stretch gently: knee-to-chest, hip and hamstring stretches, held steadily without bouncing and never into sharp pain
  • Strengthen: core and back exercises a few times a week, built up slowly
  • Sit and lift well: a small cushion in the curve of the lower back and feet flat on the floor; bend your knees to lift and keep the load close
  • Massage and relaxation: a gentle massage by a trained therapist, slow breathing and enough sleep can ease muscle tension
  • Pain relief: ask your doctor or pharmacist what is safe for you; do not start new medicine on your own

Tell your therapist

  • Numb or tingling leg or foot
  • Recent back injury or surgery
  • Weak bones
  • Pacemaker or metal implant
  • Pregnancy
  • Blood-thinning medicine

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
