Pressure sore (bedsore)

Damaged skin and tissue caused by lasting pressure on one spot, often over bony parts like the hips or heels

ConditionSkin, hair & nailsSkin

What it is

A pressure sore starts when pressure or rubbing on one area of skin lasts too long and cuts down blood flow, so the skin turns red and may break open into a sore.

  • Hips, tailbone, heels
  • Long time in bed or chair
  • Red skin, then a sore
Which doctor
Family doctor or nurse; a wound-care team for deep sores
Common in
Older or frail people, and anyone who cannot easily move or change position
Contagious
No
Course
Early redness may fade once pressure is off; open sores can take weeks to months

Common signs

  • A red area of skin that does not turn pale when pressed
  • Skin that feels warmer, firmer or softer than the skin around it
  • Pain, tenderness or itching over a bony area, or no feeling there at all
  • A blister or an open sore
  • Deeper sores can look like a crater, sometimes with dark or black skin

Why it happens

  • Staying in one position for a long time, in bed or in a chair or wheelchair
  • Skin dragged or rubbed against sheets or a chair, for example when sliding down in bed
  • Skin kept damp by sweat, urine or stool, which makes it weaker
  • Less feeling in the skin or trouble moving, for example after a stroke or spinal injury
  • Poor food or fluid intake, diabetes, poor blood flow and older age raise the risk

See a doctor first if

  • Fever, shivering, confusion or feeling very unwell with a sore: go to hospital now
  • A deep sore that shows fat, muscle or bone, or has black, dead-looking skin: see a doctor promptly
  • Pus, a bad smell, spreading redness, heat or swelling around a sore: see a doctor promptly
  • Skin that stays red when pressed, a blister or any open sore: tell a doctor or nurse soon
  • A sore that gets bigger or more painful, bleeds, or is no better after a few weeks: see a doctor

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

This page is general information. Please keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Looking after yourself

More ways to look after yourself

  • Change position often, as your nurse or doctor advises; shift your weight often when sitting
  • Keep pressure off the sore; ask your nurse about a special mattress or cushion
  • Check your skin every day, or ask a carer to, especially hips, tailbone, heels and elbows
  • Keep skin clean and dry; do not rub red areas; put nothing on an open sore unless a nurse says so

Tell your therapist

  • Broken, red or sore skin
  • Diabetes or less feeling in the skin
  • Fever or an active infection
  • Pacemaker or metal implant

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
