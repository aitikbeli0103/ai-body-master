Shattered bone (comminuted fracture)

A bone broken into three or more pieces, usually after a strong force

ConditionAny other bone or soft-tissue injuryBones & musclesWhole body

What it is

A shattered (comminuted) fracture means a bone has broken into three or more pieces, usually after a hard blow, a bad fall or a road accident, and it needs urgent medical care.

  • Any bone in the body
  • After a serious fall or crash
  • Severe pain and swelling
Which doctor
Hospital emergency team first, then a bone doctor (orthopaedic)
Common in
After serious accidents; older people with weak bones after a fall
Contagious
No
Course
Needs urgent hospital care; recovery usually takes months, with a plan from the doctor

Common signs

More signs

  • Severe pain at the injury, worse when you move or touch it
  • The limb looks bent, short or odd-shaped; a bone may show through the skin
  • A grinding feeling, or numbness and tingling below the injury

Why it happens

Other causes

  • Weak bones (osteoporosis), which can break badly even in a fall from standing

See a doctor first if

  • A suspected shattered bone: call 999 or go to hospital now
  • Bone showing through the skin, heavy bleeding or a deep wound: call 999
  • Head, neck or back injury, fainting or confusion: call 999 and do not move the person
  • A bent or odd-shaped limb, numbness, or a pale, cold or bluish hand or foot: go to hospital now
  • After hospital care: fever, pus, or swelling and pain getting worse: see a doctor now
  • A painful, swollen calf after an injury (possible clot): see a doctor now; chest pain or breathlessness: call 999

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

This page is general information. Please keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Looking after yourself

More ways to look after yourself

  • Keep the injured part as still as you can, and support it in the position you found it
  • Do not try to straighten it or push a bone back in; if the skin is broken, cover it with a clean cloth
  • After hospital care, follow the care team's plan on rest, cast or support, exercises and check-ups
  • Smoking can slow bone mending, so ask for help to stop if you smoke

Tell your therapist

  • Pacemaker or metal implant
  • Weak bones
  • Blood-thinning medicine
  • A blood clot or a new swollen leg
  • Broken, red or sore skin
  • Pregnancy

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
