Slipped disc in the lower back

Also called a herniated or prolapsed lumbar disc

ConditionBones, joints & spineLower backBones & muscles

What it is

A soft disc between two bones of the lower back bulges or pushes out and can press on a nearby nerve.

  • Lower back
  • Can press on a nerve
  • Back and leg pain
Which doctor
Orthopaedic (spine) doctor, neurologist, physiotherapist
Common in
Adults aged 30 to 50, more often men
Contagious
No
Course
Often improves over weeks to months
Medical details

What happens in the lower back

Between each pair of vertebrae sits a disc: a tough outer ring around a soft, gel-like centre. With age, repeated strain or sometimes one sudden effort, the ring can weaken or crack and the centre can push out. Most herniations are at L4–L5 and L5–S1. The bulge can irritate or press on a nerve root that passes close by, which causes pain, tingling or weakness along the leg. The body often absorbs part of the herniated material over weeks to months.

From mild to severe

  • Bulging disc: the disc sticks out but the outer ring is still whole
  • Herniation: the soft centre pushes through a weak part of the ring
  • Extrusion: a piece breaks away and moves out of the disc; less common and more likely to cause strong nerve symptoms

Outlook

Most people improve without an operation within a few weeks to months, with staying gently active, physiotherapy and pain relief chosen by their doctor. A small number have severe or lasting nerve symptoms and are offered further tests or procedures. Cauda equina syndrome is rare, but it is an emergency.

Common signs

Medical details

Symptoms in more detail

  • Low back pain: sometimes sudden, often worse when bending forward, sitting, coughing or sneezing
  • Sciatica (leg pain): sharp, burning or electric pain from the buttock down the back or side of the leg, usually on one side
  • Pins and needles or numbness: in the calf, foot or toes, following the path of the nerve
  • Weakness: trouble lifting the foot or toes, tripping, or a leg that gives way
  • Warning signs: numbness around the groin or inner thighs, new trouble passing urine or controlling the bowels, or weakness in both legs: go to hospital now

Why it happens

Medical details

Causes and risk factors

Discs lose water and flexibility with age, which makes the outer ring easier to weaken. A herniation often follows years of small strains plus one more trigger, such as lifting, twisting or a long drive.

  • Age: most often between about 30 and 50, while the discs are still soft
  • Heavy or repeated lifting and twisting: especially with a rounded back
  • Long sitting and driving: sitting puts more load on the discs than standing
  • Extra body weight and smoking: more load on the discs and poorer disc health
  • Family history and weak core muscles: disc problems can run in families, and weak muscles give the spine less support

See a doctor first if

  • Numb around the groin or inner thighs: go to hospital now
  • New trouble passing urine or controlling the bowels: go to hospital now
  • A weak leg or foot, or tripping
  • Pain after a fall or accident, or with a fever
  • Severe pain that does not ease with rest or wakes you at night
  • Leg pain not easing after about 6 weeks

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Medical details

How doctors check it

Many people with no symptoms also show disc bulges on an MRI, so the scan is always read together with your symptoms and examination.

  • History and nerve examination: muscle strength, reflexes, feeling in the legs and the straight leg raise test; they show which nerve root may be involved
  • MRI: the best test to see the discs and nerves; used when symptoms are severe, last more than about 6 weeks, or surgery is considered
  • X-ray: does not show discs, but shows the bones and how they line up
  • CT scan and nerve tests (EMG): used in selected cases

Problems that can feel similar

Muscle strain, hip joint problems, sacroiliac joint pain, narrowing of the spinal canal and a tight buttock muscle pressing on the nerve can feel like a slipped disc, so a doctor checks to tell them apart. Back or leg pain with a fever, chest pain or sudden severe belly pain needs urgent care: call 999.

Looking after yourself

Medical details

Daily care in more detail

  • Stay gently active: short walks several times a day usually help more than long bed rest; stop any movement that sends pain or tingling down the leg
  • Pain relief: ask your doctor or pharmacist what is safe for you; do not start new medicine on your own
  • Physiotherapy: a physiotherapist can teach exercises that suit your disc and nerve symptoms; core exercises and stretches are added once the pain settles
  • Sit and lift well: a small cushion in the curve of the lower back, change position often, bend your knees to lift and keep the load close
  • Warmth: a warm pack can ease the tight muscles around the spine
  • Weight and smoking: a healthy weight and not smoking are good for the discs

Tell your therapist

  • Numb or tingling leg or foot
  • Weak leg or foot
  • Recent back injury or surgery
  • Weak bones
  • Pacemaker or metal implant
  • Pregnancy

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
