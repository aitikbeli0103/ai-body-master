Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN)

Abnormal changes in the cells that line the cervix, usually from a long-lasting HPV infection

ConditionWomen's healthReproductive

What it is

CIN is a change in the cells on the surface of the cervix that can be seen on tests. It is not cancer, but some changes can turn into cancer over time if they are not followed up.

  • Cells on the cervix surface
  • Linked to long-term HPV
  • Often no signs; seen on tests
Which doctor
Women's doctor (gynaecologist); family doctor for screening
Common in
Adult women, most often found at screening tests
Contagious
No, but HPV (the usual cause) can pass on through sexual contact
Course
Mild changes may go away by themselves; a doctor will plan check-ups and next steps

Common signs

More signs

  • Found by a Pap smear or HPV test during screening
  • A closer look at the cervix (colposcopy) may be advised after an abnormal result

Why it happens

Other causes

  • More exposure to HPV, for example starting sex young or having several partners
  • Not having regular screening means changes are found later

See a doctor first if

  • Heavy vaginal bleeding, or bleeding with dizziness or fainting: go to hospital now
  • Bleeding after sex or between periods, or any bleeding after menopause
  • Unusual discharge that is bloody, watery or bad-smelling
  • Pain in the lower belly, pelvis or lower back that does not go away
  • An abnormal Pap smear or HPV result: do not wait, book your follow-up
  • A follow-up check you have missed: book it again soon

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

Looking after yourself

More ways to look after yourself

  • If you smoke, stopping is good for your cervix and your general health
  • Condoms can lower the chance of catching or passing on HPV, but do not protect fully
  • Feeling worried is normal; talk to your doctor or someone you trust

Tell your therapist

  • Pregnancy
  • Blood-thinning medicine
  • Fever or an active infection
  • Pacemaker or metal implant
