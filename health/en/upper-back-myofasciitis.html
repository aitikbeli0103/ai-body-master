Upper back myofasciitis

Aching, tightness or burning between the shoulder blades from irritated back muscles and the tissue around them

ConditionBones, joints & spineBack

What it is

Aching, tightness or a burning feeling in the muscles and tissue around the shoulder blades and the upper spine, usually from long sitting, overuse or tension.

  • Upper back
  • Often with desk work
  • Aching, tight band
Which doctor
Family doctor, physiotherapist, orthopaedic or rehabilitation doctor
Common in
Office workers and people who sit for long hours
Contagious
No
Course
Comes and goes, often improves over weeks with better posture and regular movement
Medical details

What happens in the upper back

The upper and middle back has several layers of muscle: the muscles that join the spine to the shoulder blades (the rhomboids and the middle and lower trapezius), the muscles that run along each side of the spine, and the thin sheet of connective tissue (fascia) that wraps them. When these muscles are overloaded for a long time, for example by sitting with the head forward and the shoulders rounded, they stay partly tight. Tight bands and tender knots (myofascial trigger points) can form. They can feel deep, burning or like a band across the back, and they can send an ache to the shoulder, the neck or the side of the chest wall. The name myofasciitis suggests inflammation, but it is not an infection: in most people the main problem is overloaded, irritated muscle and fascia. The bones and nerves are usually not the main problem.

Common patterns

  • Posture and overuse: long hours at a desk or a screen, head forward, shoulders rounded; the ache builds up during the day
  • Cold and stress related: a cold draught or air-conditioning on the back, and stress that keeps the shoulders tense, can set it off
  • After a strain: a sudden lift, a twist, sports or a fall can leave the muscles sore and tight for weeks

Outlook

Most people improve over several weeks with better posture, regular movement, gentle stretching and strengthening of the back muscles. It can come back if the habits that set it off stay the same. If the pain does not settle, a doctor or physiotherapist can look for other causes. Treatment decisions are made by your doctor.

Common signs

Medical details

Symptoms in more detail

  • Deep ache or a tight band: a dull, deep ache or a feeling of a tight band across the upper back, mostly between the shoulder blades and beside the spine
  • Burning ache: some people feel a burning or raw ache between the shoulder blades
  • Tender knots: small, tight, tender spots in the muscle that hurt when touched and can send pain to the shoulder, the neck, the upper arm or the side of the chest wall
  • Worse with movement: twisting, reaching forward or overhead, and sometimes a deep breath make it worse because the muscles around the ribs and shoulder blades are working
  • Time pattern: worse at the end of the workday, after long sitting and in cold weather; often easier with warmth and gentle movement
  • Not typical: chest pain or pressure, breathlessness, a fever, or numbness or weakness in the arms or legs need a doctor's check

Why it happens

Medical details

Causes and risk factors

  • Long sitting and poor posture: hours at a desk with the head forward and the shoulders rounded keep the upper back muscles working without rest
  • Repetitive work and carrying: typing, driving, working with the arms overhead, and carrying a heavy bag on one shoulder strain one side of the upper back
  • Cold air: air-conditioning or a draught blowing on the back, or sleeping with the back exposed, can make muscles tighten
  • Sudden strain or injury: a heavy lift, a twist, a sports injury or a fall
  • Little exercise: weak back and shoulder blade muscles tire early
  • Stress and poor sleep: they keep the shoulders tense and slow recovery

See a doctor first if

  • Pain with chest tightness, pressure, sweating or breathlessness: call 999
  • Pain with a fever, a new cough or breathlessness
  • A burning pain followed by a blistering rash on one side of the body
  • Pain after a fall or an accident
  • New numbness or weakness in the legs, or trouble with the bladder or bowels
  • Severe pain that wakes you at night, unexplained weight loss, or pain that does not ease after several weeks

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Medical details

How doctors check it

Tender knots (trigger points) are found by touch, so the diagnosis is mostly made from your story and the examination.

  • History and examination: when the pain started, what makes it better or worse, your work and habits, and any chest, breathing, stomach or fever symptoms; then your posture, the movement of the spine, shoulders and shoulder blades, tender muscles and tight bands, and a check of the nerves, heart and lungs when needed
  • Tests: most people with a typical muscle pain do not need any; an X-ray, blood tests, an ECG, a chest X-ray or an MRI are used when there are warning signs or the symptoms do not fit a muscle problem

Problems that can feel similar

Heart problems (such as angina or a heart attack), lung problems (such as pneumonia, inflammation of the lining of the lung or a blood clot in the lung), gallbladder problems (the pain can be felt between the shoulder blades), shingles before the rash comes out, and problems of the ribs or the thoracic spine can feel like upper back muscle pain, so a doctor checks to tell them apart.

Pain with chest tightness, sweating or breathlessness can be a heart problem: call emergency services (999).

Looking after yourself

Medical details

Daily care in more detail

  • Workstation: top of the screen at eye level, about an arm's length away; keyboard close; elbows at about 90 degrees; a chair that supports your back; shoulders relaxed, not shrugged
  • Movement breaks: stand up and move every 30 to 45 minutes; roll the shoulders back and gently bring the shoulder blades together
  • Gentle stretches: opening the chest in a doorway and gently arching the upper back over the back of a chair, only within a comfortable range; a physiotherapist can show you which ones suit you
  • Strength and exercise: regular walking or swimming, and strengthening of the shoulder blade, upper back and core muscles
  • Warmth and relaxation: a warm pack or a warm shower can ease tight muscles; keep air-conditioning and fans from blowing on your back; slow breathing and short pauses help the shoulders let go
  • Easing tender knots: slow, gentle self-massage with a soft ball against a wall can feel relieving for short moments; stop if it hurts more, and ask a physiotherapist to show you
  • Pain relief: ask your doctor or pharmacist what is safe for you; do not start new medicine on your own

Tell your therapist

  • Numb or tingling arm
  • Recent back injury or surgery
  • Weak bones
  • Pacemaker or metal implant
  • Pregnancy
