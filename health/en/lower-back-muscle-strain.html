Lower-back muscle strain

Also called chronic lumbar strain or lumbar muscle fatigue

Medical details

What happens in the lower back

The lower back (lumbar spine) has five vertebrae, L1 to L5, held together by discs, joints, ligaments and layers of muscle. The muscles along the spine and around the core hold your upper body up all day. When they are overloaded again and again, or kept in one position for hours, small areas of the muscle and the thin sheet of fascia around it become tight and tender, and tiny strains build up. The pain is usually a dull ache across the lower back that eases with gentle movement and warmth. Scans are often normal: this is a problem of muscle and soft tissue, not of a damaged bone or disc.

Common patterns

  • Acute: a sudden overload, such as lifting or twisting; it usually settles over days to a few weeks
  • Chronic: repeated or constant overload; the ache lasts longer than about 3 months and comes and goes with activity
  • Trigger points: tight, tender knots in a muscle that can send an ache into the buttock or hip

Outlook

Most lower-back muscle strain improves with time, staying active, good posture and strengthening exercises. Long rest in bed tends to make it worse. It can come back if the habits that cause it stay the same. Treatment decisions are made by your doctor.

Common signs

Medical details

Symptoms in more detail

  • Dull ache: a deep ache across the lower back, sometimes more on one side; often worse at the end of the day or after sitting, standing or bending for long
  • Stiffness: the back feels stiff in the morning or after sitting for a while, and loosens once you start moving
  • Tender spots: the muscles beside the spine feel sore when pressed; some people feel tight knots
  • Limited bending: bending forward or twisting is limited by tightness and discomfort
  • Not typical of simple muscle strain: numbness, weakness in the legs, or pain that shoots below the knee. These need a doctor's check

Why it happens

Medical details

Causes and risk factors

The common thread is a load the muscles cannot keep up with: too much (lifting, twisting), too long (sitting or standing for hours) or too weak (little exercise, so the core and back muscles tire easily). Poor posture moves the load onto the same few muscles again and again.

  • Lifting and twisting: especially with a rounded back or with the load far from the body
  • Long sitting or standing: desk work, driving, or standing at work without breaks
  • Weak core and back muscles: little exercise means the muscles tire early
  • Poor posture: slouching, or sitting and sleeping in positions that strain the back
  • Extra body weight, smoking, stress and poor sleep: linked with more back pain in studies

See a doctor first if

  • Back pain after a fall, accident or hard knock
  • Back pain with a fever or unexplained weight loss
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the legs
  • Cannot control bladder or bowels, or numb around the groin: go to hospital now
  • Severe pain that wakes you at night or does not ease when you lie down
  • Pain not easing after a few weeks

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Medical details

How doctors check it

Scans often show changes such as disc wear in people with no pain at all, so results are always read together with your symptoms.

  • History and examination: where and when it hurts, what makes it better or worse, your work and activity; the doctor checks posture, spine movement, muscle tightness, leg strength, reflexes and feeling
  • Nerve tests: such as the straight leg raise test; they help tell muscle pain from a nerve problem
  • X-ray, MRI or CT: not needed for most people with ordinary muscle strain; used when there are warning signs, nerve symptoms or pain that does not improve
  • Blood or urine tests: only if an infection or an inflammatory disease is suspected

Problems that can feel similar

A slipped disc, wear and tear of the spine, a kidney or urine infection, and problems of the hip or the sacroiliac joint can all cause lower-back pain, so a doctor checks to tell them apart. Back pain with a fever, chest pain or sudden severe belly pain needs urgent medical care: call 999.

Looking after yourself

Medical details

Daily care in more detail

  • Keep moving: gentle walking and normal daily activity help more than long bed rest; start small and build up
  • Strengthen: core and back exercises a few times a week, built up slowly; stop if they cause sharp pain
  • Stretch gently: hamstrings, hips and lower back, held without bouncing, a few times a day
  • Sit well: feet flat, a small cushion in the curve of the lower back, and stand up and move every 30 to 45 minutes
  • Lift safely: bend your knees, keep the load close and avoid twisting while lifting
  • Warmth and sleep: a warm pack can ease tight muscles; sleep on your side with a pillow between the knees, or on your back with a pillow under the knees
  • Weight, smoking and stress: a healthy weight, not smoking and managing stress all help the back

Tell your therapist

  • Numb or tingling leg or foot
  • Recent back injury or surgery
  • Weak bones
  • Pacemaker or metal implant
  • Pregnancy
  • Blood-thinning medicine
