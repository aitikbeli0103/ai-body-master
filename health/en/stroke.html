Stroke

A sudden problem with blood supply to the brain, when a vessel is blocked or bursts

ConditionBrain & nervesHead

What it is

A stroke happens when blood cannot reach part of the brain because a vessel is blocked or bursts. It is an emergency: getting help fast matters. Call 999 at the first sign.

  • The brain
  • More common with age
  • Sudden weakness, speech trouble
Which doctor
Call 999 first; later a brain and nerve doctor (neurologist)
Common in
Older adults; people with high blood pressure, diabetes, heart problems or who smoke
Contagious
No
Course
Needs emergency care; recovery differs for each person and may take months

Common signs

  • Face drooping on one side, or a lopsided smile
  • Weakness or numbness in one arm or leg, often on one side of the body
  • Slurred speech, trouble finding words or trouble understanding others
  • Sudden blurred or lost vision in one or both eyes, or double vision
  • Sudden dizziness, loss of balance, or a very bad headache with no known cause

Why it happens

  • A blood clot blocks a vessel in the brain; this is the more common type
  • A blood vessel in the brain bursts and bleeds
  • High blood pressure is one of the main risks
  • Diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, extra weight and little exercise
  • Heart problems, especially an uneven heartbeat, and getting older

See a doctor first if

  • Face drooping, arm weakness or speech trouble: call 999 at once
  • Sudden severe headache, loss of vision, loss of balance or confusion: call 999
  • Signs that pass in minutes (a mini-stroke) are still an emergency: go to hospital now
  • Do not wait or drive yourself; note the time the signs began and tell the ambulance team
  • Very drowsy, hard to wake, a fit, or trouble breathing: call 999
  • After a stroke: choking when eating, a fever or a bad fall: see a doctor the same day

If any apply, please see a doctor before a session.

Keep your normal care

Keep taking your normal medicine and keep seeing your doctor. Our session is a gentle support and does not replace medical care.

Looking after yourself

  • Follow your doctor's plan, take medicines as prescribed and never stop them on your own
  • Keep blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol under control, with regular check-ups
  • Do not smoke, limit alcohol, and eat less salt and more vegetables and fruit
  • Stay gently active and keep to a steady weight, as your doctor allows
  • After a stroke, ask about rehabilitation, such as physiotherapy and speech help

Tell your therapist

  • High blood pressure
  • A heart condition
  • Blood-thinning medicine
  • Diabetes or less feeling in the skin
  • Weak leg or foot
  • Numb or tingling arm

Your therapist will go through this with you before starting.
